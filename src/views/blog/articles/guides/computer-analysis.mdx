**Computer analysis** runs the engine over every move of a game and writes down what it finds, the way lichess's *Request a computer analysis* does: an evaluation after each move, the inaccuracies, mistakes and blunders marked, the move that was better beside each one, and a summary per player with their **accuracy**.

It runs as a **background job**. You start it from the Analysis Board, then keep using the app — another game, another screen — while it works. When it is done, the result is one or more new **saved analyses**: ordinary PGNs you open, study, edit and export like any other.

## In short

1. Open a game on the [Analysis Board](/tools/analysis).
2. On its **Computer analysis** tab, check the options and tick the outputs you want: **light**, **medium** and/or **full**.
3. Press **Start**. Watch the progress in the header, or on the **Jobs** screen.
4. When it finishes, open the new saved analysis. The report and the evaluation graph are on the board.

## Starting an analysis

Any game the Analysis Board holds can be analysed: one you saved, one from the Library, a game you played against the engine, or a PGN you just pasted. The job analyses the game's **main line**. Its side lines and comments are kept, but not judged.

### Which engine

The job uses the engine chosen in [Settings → Engine](/settings/engine): one of the builds that run in the browser, or — much faster — a native Stockfish on your own computer through the engine server (see [Run a chess engine on your own computer](/blog/guides/local-engine)). It starts its own copy of the engine, separate from any board's.

**Threads** and **hash** are held to what that engine accepts. The browser's single-thread build always runs on one thread, and the form says so.

### How long it takes

Roughly the number of positions times the time each one takes. In the browser's single-thread build on a fast desktop, depth 20 takes a few seconds a position, so a 40-move game — about 80 positions — takes several minutes. More lines, more depth or a slower machine take longer; the engine server with several threads is many times faster.

## The options

| Option | Default | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Engine** | your choice in Settings | Which engine searches. |
| **Threads** | 1 | CPU threads for the engine, up to what it accepts. |
| **Hash** | 64 MB | The engine's memory for positions it has seen. |
| **Depth** | 20 | How many plies deep each position is searched. Deeper is more reliable and slower. |
| **Time per move** | 30 s | The most time one position may take. The search stops at the depth or the time, whichever comes first. 0 means no time limit. |
| **Lines** | 1 | How many candidate moves the engine works out in each position (MultiPV), 1–10. More lines mean more alternatives in the medium and full outputs, and a slower search. |
| **Early stop from depth** | the depth | Once the search reaches this depth, it may stop as soon as one of the lines is clearly worse than the best (below). At most the depth; at the depth itself, it never stops early. |
| **Variation range** | 100 cp | How far below the best line another line may be and still count as a real alternative, in centipawns (100 cp = one pawn). |
| **Analyse** | both sides | Only White's moves, only Black's, or both. |
| **From move** | 1, White | The first move analysed: its number and colour. |
| **To move** | the end | The last move number analysed, both colours. |
| **Inaccuracy / Mistake / Blunder** | 50 / 100 / 300 cp | How much a move must give up to be marked as each. |
| **Outputs** | light | Which annotated PGNs to write: light, medium and/or full. One engine run makes all of them. |

The moves outside *Analyse*, *From move* and *To move* stay in the game, unmarked, and are left out of the report.

## How a move is judged

### What is searched

For each move, the engine looks at two positions: the one **before** the move, to find the best move there, and the one **after** it, to see what the move played actually left. The position after one move is the position before the next, so each position is searched once — the whole range of the game, from the position before the first analysed move to the position after the last.

A position whose own move is not analysed (Black's, when you analyse White only) is searched for one line only: only its evaluation is needed, as the result of the move before it. A checkmate or stalemate on the board is not searched at all.

Evaluations the game **already has** — from an earlier analysis, or a lichess export — are used rather than recalculated when they are at least as deep as the depth you asked for.

### The search, and the lines kept

All scores are in **centipawns**: hundredths of a pawn, from White's point of view (+1.50 is White a pawn and a half better). A forced mate counts as 10,000 less the number of moves to it, so a mate in 2 beats a mate in 5, and both beat any material advantage.

With more than one line, two rules keep the job fast and the output useful:

- **The early stop.** Once the search reaches the *early stop* depth, it stops as soon as any line falls more than the *variation range* below the best line — from the point of view of the side to move. The best move is then clear enough.
- **The cut.** The lines kept are the best one and every line after it **up to the first** that is more than the *variation range* below it. Weaker lines are dropped.

### The loss

A move's **loss** is how much worse the position got for the player who moved:

> loss = the best line's evaluation before the move − the evaluation after the move

measured for the player who moved, with each evaluation held between −10 and +10 pawns first (a mate counts as ±10). A move that keeps the position as good as the best line, or better, loses nothing. Even the engine's own choice can show a small loss: the position after it is searched afresh, and a search that sees one move further sometimes sees a little less.

| Loss | The move is |
| --- | --- |
| more than 300 cp | a **blunder** (??) |
| more than 100 cp | a **mistake** (?) |
| more than 50 cp | an **inaccuracy** (?!) |
| 50 cp or less | fine — no mark |

Those are the default thresholds; the form changes them.

### A missed mate

When the player had a **forced mate** and the move gave it up — the evaluation after it is no longer a mate for them, it is a mate for the opponent, or it is a **longer** mate — the move is a **missed mate**. It is marked ?? and says how short the mate was: *Missed mate in 2! Qxf7# was best.* A missed mate has no loss in centipawns, so it does not change the average below. A move that **delivers** mate is never a missed mate.

### The better move

Beside each marked move, the analysis names **the best move that was not played**: the first kept line whose first move differs from the move on the board — *Mistake. Nf3 was best.*

## The report

For each player, over the moves analysed for them:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Inaccuracies, mistakes, blunders, missed mates** | How many of each. |
| **ACPL** | Average centipawn loss: the losses added up and divided by the number of moves, rounded. Lower is better; strong players' games often come in under 20. |
| **Accuracy** | Lichess's formula: 103.1668 × e^(−0.004354 × ACPL) − 3.1669, held between 0 and 100%. |

How ACPL turns into accuracy:

| ACPL | 0 | 10 | 25 | 50 | 100 | 200 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy | 100% | 96% | 89% | 80% | 64% | 40% |

A player with no analysed move — Black, when you analyse White only — has no report.

### The evaluation graph

One point per evaluated move: White's advantage above the middle, Black's below, held at ±10 pawns, the marked moves picked out. **Click a point** to take the board to that move. The same report and graph are on the job's entry on the Jobs screen.

## The three outputs

All three come from the **same engine run**, so ticking all of them costs no more time than one. Each is saved as its own analysis, in the same folder as the game it came from (*Unfiled* if it had none), named *&lt;the game's name&gt; — computer analysis (light)* and so on.

| | light | medium | full |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Evaluation on every move | yes | yes | yes |
| Marks and comments on the mistakes | yes | yes | yes |
| Engine lines added as side lines | the best other move | every kept line | every kept line |
| …on which moves | the marked ones | the marked ones | **every** analysed move |
| Size | small | medium | large |

- **Light** is for reading: the game, its mistakes, and for each one the move that was better.
- **Medium** shows every good alternative at the moments that mattered.
- **Full** is for study: the engine's lines at every move. With several lines it grows quickly.

Every output keeps everything the game already had — your own side lines, comments and annotations. The engine's lines are added beside them, and a line the game already had is followed rather than added twice.

## What is written into the PGN

An analysis is a PGN like any other — it opens in lichess, ChessBase or any other chess program — and everything the job found is in standard places.

| What | Where | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Evaluation** | `[%eval]` in the move's comment: pawns from White's side, two decimals, then the depth. A mate is `#N`, negative when Black mates. | `{ [%eval 0.35,20] }`, `{ [%eval #-2,20] }` |
| **The mark** | A NAG: `$6` ?!, `$2` ?, `$4` ?? (a missed mate too). It replaces any ! or ? the move already had; other annotations stay. | `5... d5 $2` |
| **The comment** | A comment of its own after the move's evaluation. | `{ Mistake. d6 was best. }` |
| **Engine lines** | Side lines, their first move with the line's evaluation. A line the engine sent that stops making sense is cut where it does. | `(5... d6 { [%eval 0.45,20] } 6. c3)` |
| **The engine** | The `Annotator` tag, with the output's kind. | `[Annotator "Stockfish 19 Lite [light]"]` |
| **The report** | The comment before the first move, after the start position's evaluation. | `{ Computer analysis (light), Stockfish 19 Lite, depth 20. White: 1 inaccuracy, … }` |
| **Which moves were analysed** | Two tags: the plies (half-moves, counted from the start) analysed, and the side when you analysed only one. | `[AnalysedPlies "1-80"]`, `[AnalysedSide "White"]` |

A move outside the analysis — Black's, when you analysed only White — still carries its **evaluation**, because the next move is measured from it. It gets nothing else: no mark, comment or line. The two tags tell the app which moves count in the report.

Analysing an analysis again replaces the marks, comments, report and tags the previous run wrote, rather than adding a second set. An evaluation the game already had at a greater depth is kept.

## Reports from any analysed game

The report and the graph are read **from the PGN itself**, not from the job — so they show for any analysed game the app holds, long after the job is gone, and for games analysed elsewhere:

- **A lichess export** with evaluations (lichess's *Download annotated PGN*): its `[%eval]`s and its ?! ? ?? marks give the same report and graph. Without the two tags, every move with an evaluation counts. The first move has no evaluation before it in a lichess export, so it counts as a move but not in the ACPL.
- **An analysis you edited**: the report follows what is in the game — remove a mark and that move is no longer counted as a mistake.

The marks come from the NAGs and the losses from the evaluations; a ?? whose evaluations show a mate given up is counted as a missed mate.

## Good to know

- **Depth matters more than lines** for the marks: every mark compares two evaluations, and a shallow search can call a sound move a mistake. Keep depth 18 or more for a game you care about.
- **More lines are for the medium and full outputs.** With one line, medium is the same as light.
- **The early stop** only helps with several lines. Set it a few plies below the depth to save time on positions where the best move is obvious.
- **Analyse one side** to study your own moves only. The other side's positions are searched for one line, which saves time with several lines.
- **Big games, big output**: full with five lines on an 80-move game adds hundreds of side-line moves. Light is the one to share.
